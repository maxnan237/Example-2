# McDonald's Corporation (MCD) — Bear Case

*Analysis date: 2026-07-02*

---

## 1. Thesis Killer

MCD's entire economic model depends on franchisee sales growth (95% of the system is franchised, and MCD's rent+royalty revenue is a direct function of franchisee top-line, per [[Business Overview]]). If the reported low-income consumer pullback (Note: from external source — MCD's own 10-K does not quantify this by income cohort) proves structural rather than cyclical — driven by a durable shift in value-conscious dining behavior rather than a temporary macro blip — franchisee sales growth slows, comparable sales stagnate, and the stable, predictable rent/royalty cash-flow stream that underpins the entire valuation case starts compounding more slowly than the market has priced in. The clearest early-warning signal already visible in the filed numbers is the two-year decline in after-tax ROIC (25.2% → 21.8% → 20.3%, [[Financial Deep Dive]]) — capital is being deployed into unit growth and international expansion at a moment when incremental returns are already falling, which is precisely the wrong combination if underlying demand is softening rather than accelerating.

---

## 2. Structural Weaknesses

### 2a. Revenue is entirely a function of franchisee health, which MCD does not control day-to-day
[MCD_10K_FY2025.htm, Risk Factors]: "Our success as a heavily franchised business relies to a large degree on the financial success and cooperation of our franchisees... The revenues we realize from franchised restaurants are largely dependent on the ability of our franchisees to grow their sales." MCD cannot directly manage the customer experience, pricing execution, or local marketing effectiveness at 95% of its restaurants — it can only influence franchisees through contract terms and system-wide strategy.

### 2b. Negative shareholders' equity limits balance-sheet flexibility
[MCD_10K_FY2025.htm, Consolidated Balance Sheet]: total shareholders' equity (deficit) was $(1,791)M at FY2025 year-end — narrower than the $(4,707)M deficit in FY2023, but still negative. Total debt of $39,973M against this equity base means MCD's capital structure has essentially no cushion from a traditional book-equity standpoint; the entire enterprise value rests on the market's confidence in future franchise cash flows, not on a tangible net-asset floor.

### 2c. Declining ROIC amid rising capex
[MCD_10K_FY2025.htm]: after-tax ROIC fell from 25.2% (2023) to 20.3% (2025) even as capital expenditures rose from $2,357M to $3,365M over the same period — the company is investing more capital for lower marginal returns, a pattern that, if it continues, will compress the very cash-generation efficiency that justifies MCD's premium historical valuation.

---

## 3. Assumptions the Bull Case Takes for Granted

1. **That the low-income consumer pullback is cyclical, not structural.** The "McDonald's > NEXT" strategy (Note: from external source) bets that value-menu repositioning can win back price-sensitive traffic — but if QSR value perception has permanently shifted (e.g., toward grocery/at-home alternatives, or toward even-cheaper regional competitors), the strategy may only slow, not reverse, the trend.

2. **That the Q2 2026 comparable-sales deceleration management has already guided to (Note: from external source) is a one-quarter comparison effect, not the start of a broader slowdown.** This will be directly tested by the July 29, 2026 earnings release.

3. **That rising International Operated Markets capex ($1,340M→$2,048M over two years) will restore ROIC rather than further dilute it.** The bull case assumes these investments are high-return; the two-year ROIC trend does not yet support that assumption.

4. **That the sharp FY2025 buyback slowdown (11.1M shares/$3.1B in FY2023 → 6.7M shares/$2.0B in FY2025, despite a new $15bn authorization) is capital discipline, not a signal that management itself is cautious about near-term cash generation.**

5. **That sell-side consensus price targets (average $329-354, per external sources) are appropriately weighting the consumer-traffic and ROIC concerns**, rather than lagging behind them — the same pattern of "consensus more bullish than the stock's actual trading level" was also observed in this vault's TME research, and should not automatically be read as the market being wrong.

## 4. Permanent Impairment Scenarios

### Scenario A: Structural trade-down away from traditional QSR
Value-conscious consumers permanently shift spending toward grocery/at-home meal prep, discount retailers' prepared foods, or ultra-low-cost regional QSR chains not captured in MCD's disclosed competitive set (which names no specific competitors — see [[Industry Scan]]). Comparable sales growth stalls across the U.S. segment specifically, compressing the highest-margin segment (53.6% operating margin) and disproportionately hurting consolidated profitability.

### Scenario B: Franchisee financial distress cascades
If comparable sales stagnate for a sustained period, thinly-capitalized franchisees could face covenant pressure on their own restaurant-level debt, leading to closures, deferred remodels, or renegotiation demands on rent/royalty terms at lease renewal — directly threatening the $31.5bn of contracted future minimum rents MCD has on its books ([[Business Overview]]). This is a slow-moving but structurally serious risk given MCD's near-total dependence on franchisee economics.

### Scenario C: Capital misallocation compounds
If the ROIC decline (25.2%→20.3%) continues for several more years as capex keeps rising to fund the 50,000-restaurant-by-2027 target, MCD could find itself over-built relative to demand in certain markets, forcing future write-downs or restaurant closures (2025 already saw 396 closures against 2,276 openings, per [[Business Overview]]) — a permanent destruction of the capital deployed into those units.

## Summary

MCD's moat (franchisee lock-in, brand, real estate, scale) is genuinely durable, but durability of structure does not guarantee durability of growth. The bear case here is not a moat-collapse story — it's a demand-and-capital-efficiency story: reported softness among low-income consumers, two straight years of declining ROIC despite rising capex, and a sharp pullback in buyback execution all point in the same direction — management deploying more capital into a system that is currently generating lower returns, at the exact moment end-consumer demand (per external reporting, not the company's own quantified disclosure) is under pressure. None of this threatens MCD's survival; it threatens the multiple and growth-rate assumptions embedded in a valuation that, even after the recent selloff, still prices in continued compounding at historical rates.
