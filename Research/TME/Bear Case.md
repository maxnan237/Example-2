# Tencent Music Entertainment Group (TME) — Bear Case

*Analysis date: 2026-07-02*

---

## 1. Thesis Killer

TME's monetization gains (paying ratio 17.1% → 22.9%, ARPPU +18% over FY2023-FY2025, per [TME_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 5]) have been masking a shrinking user base (MAU 589m → 547m, -7.1% over the same period). If a well-capitalized new entrant — reported externally as ByteDance's Soda Music, leveraging Douyin's short-video distribution and reported to have already reached 140m+ MAU (Note: from external source, not disclosed in TME's own filings) — continues taking share, TME's future monetization gains have a shrinking base to compound against. Because content licensing is explicitly non-exclusive ([TME_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.B]), TME has no structural way to lock users in or lock competitors out of the same catalog. The single most likely way a long-term holder loses money here is a continued MAU decline that eventually overwhelms ARPPU growth, compounded by a market that can no longer verify the trend quarter-to-quarter because TME has stopped disclosing MAU/paying-user/ARPPU data on a quarterly basis (Note: from external source) starting Q1 2026.

## 2. Structural Weaknesses

### 2a. Non-exclusive content licensing
[TME_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.B]: "Our licensing agreements with music labels and music copyright owners are on a non-exclusive basis." TME's core product input — its music catalog — is licensable by any competitor with sufficient capital, including NetEase Cloud Music today and, per external reporting, ByteDance's Soda Music. There is no content moat to defend share with; TME must compete on distribution, UX, and price, all of which are more contestable than exclusive content.

### 2b. Controlling shareholder with misaligned-interest risk
[TME_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 3.D. Risk Factors]: "Tencent, our controlling shareholder, has had and will continue to have effective control over the outcome of shareholder actions in our company. The interests of Tencent may not be aligned with the interests of our other shareholders and holders of the ADSs." (Note: from external source) Tencent holds >90% of voting power. Minority ADS holders cannot influence major strategic or capital-allocation decisions, including large ones like the Ximalaya acquisition or future related-party transactions with Tencent's broader ecosystem.

### 2c. VIE structure — economic interest via contract, not ownership
[TME_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.C]: "the Group primarily conducts its business in China through the VIEs... based on a series of contractual arrangements... These contractual arrangements may not be as effective as direct ownership in providing us with control over the VIEs." Combined with [Item 3.D]: "If the PRC regulators find such agreements non-compliant with relevant PRC laws, regulations, and rules... we could be subject to severe penalties or be forced to relinquish our interests in the VIEs." ADS holders' claim on TME's actual licensed operating businesses is legally indirect — a durable structural risk unique to the China-ADR asset class, not specific to TME's operating performance.

## 3. Assumptions the Bull Case Takes for Granted

1. **That MAU decline stabilizes rather than accelerates.** The bull/base scenarios in [[Valuation Check]] both require this. Two consecutive years of decline (589m → 570m → 547m) with a well-funded new competitor entering is not obviously a stabilizing trend — it might just as easily be an accelerating one, and the market currently has no quarterly disclosure to check.

2. **That ARPPU growth can continue indefinitely to offset user loss.** ARPPU rose from RMB10.0 to RMB11.8/month (2023-2025), an 18% gain — but there is a ceiling to how much Chinese consumers will pay for music subscriptions relative to disposable income, and continued price/tier increases risk accelerating churn among price-sensitive users, especially with a free/ad-supported competitor (Soda Music, per external reporting) gaining share.

3. **That the Ximalaya acquisition strengthens rather than dilutes the core business.** A US$1.26bn cash-plus-share deal, funded partly with new debt (RMB3.0bn drawn Q1 2026, [TME_20F_FY2025.htm, Note 35]) for the first time in years despite a net-cash balance sheet, assumes successful integration of a large standalone platform under SAMR-imposed restrictions (no price increases, no new exclusive licensing, per external reporting) — conditions that constrain exactly the kind of moat-widening moves a bull case would want management to make.

4. **That the NetEase antitrust litigation resolves without a structural remedy.** [TME_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 8]: "We are currently unable to estimate the potential loss, if any." A structural remedy (e.g., a forced change to licensing practices, similar in spirit to the SAMR conditions already imposed on Ximalaya) could permanently reduce whatever residual licensing leverage TME retains.

5. **That the sell-side average price target (~$16, per external sources) reflects updated, not stale, competitive assumptions.** Consensus targets were largely set before or shortly after the March 2026 MAU-decline shock and may not fully reflect the Soda Music threat, which is not named anywhere in TME's own regulatory filings and may be underweighted in analyst models that lean on company disclosure.

## 4. Permanent Impairment Scenarios

### Scenario A: Soda Music-driven share loss becomes structural
ByteDance leverages Douyin's short-video distribution to permanently capture the next generation of Chinese music listeners, particularly younger users who discover music via short-video first. TME's MAU decline accelerates rather than stabilizes; paying-user growth eventually rolls over as the addressable base shrinks faster than conversion/ARPPU gains can offset. Given non-exclusive licensing, TME has no defensive lever except price/UX competition against a company with a demonstrably larger distribution funnel.

### Scenario B: Regulatory action forces a structural change to licensing economics
The NetEase antitrust suit, or a broader SAMR/anti-monopoly review triggered by the Ximalaya deal, results in a structural remedy — e.g., mandatory licensing terms, restrictions on bundling, or a ban extending beyond the Ximalaya-specific SAMR conditions on new exclusive deals. This would permanently cap TME's ability to use content or M&A to defend share, leaving distribution (the Tencent relationship) as the only remaining lever — one Tencent itself controls, not TME's independent management.

### Scenario C: VIE/ADS structural risk crystallizes
A future shift in PRC or U.S. regulatory posture toward the VIE structure (either from Beijing restricting the arrangement, or from Washington tightening HFCAA/CSRC-related rules for China-based issuers) impairs ADS holders' ability to realize economic value from the underlying PRC operating entities, independent of TME's operating performance. The HKEX secondary listing (code 1698) partially mitigates a pure-NYSE-delisting scenario, but does not address the underlying VIE-ownership risk itself.

## Summary

TME is a China-only, single-market bet on a business whose core content input is explicitly non-exclusive and whose largest named competitive threat (a new, well-capitalized entrant reportedly using short-video distribution to win younger users) does not even appear in the company's own risk factor disclosures. Two years of MAU decline have been offset by rising ARPPU and paying-user conversion, but that offsetting dynamic has a ceiling, and the market's ability to monitor whether it is stabilizing or breaking down has just gotten worse, not better, with the discontinuation of quarterly user-metric disclosure. Layer on a controlling shareholder with unchecked voting power, a VIE structure that makes ADS holders' economic claim indirect, an unresolved antitrust suit from the company's most direct competitor, and freshly-imposed regulatory restrictions on TME's own ability to pursue new exclusive licensing deals, and the bear case is not a tail risk — it is a plausible continuation of trends already visible in the filed numbers.
