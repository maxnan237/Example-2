# Seanergy Maritime Holdings Corp. (SHIP) — Business Overview

*Analysis date: 2026-07-02 | Primary source: Form 20-F (fiscal year 2025, filed March 2026)*

---

## 1. What the Company Does

[SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.A]: "We are an international shipping company specializing in the worldwide seaborne transportation of dry bulk commodities. We currently own or finance lease 18 Capesize dry bulk vessels and two Newcastlemax dry bulk vessels with a cargo-carrying capacity of approximately 3,633,861 dwt and an average fleet age of approximately 14.7 years. In addition, we have five newbuilding vessels under construction, four Capesize and one Newcastlemax." → SHIP is a pure-play Capesize/Newcastlemax dry bulk owner-operator, incorporated in the Marshall Islands, headquartered in Glyfada, Greece — a single-vessel-class strategy, not a diversified shipping fleet.

- **Employees:** 96 onshore support staff (Dec 31, 2025), plus 2 executive officers (Stamatios Tsantanis, CEO; Stavros Gyftakis) and general counsel Theodora Mitropetrou [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.D]. Seafarers are not direct employees — crewing is outsourced to third-party managers (V.Ships Greece, Global Seaways, Navilands); Seanergy Shipmanagement (wholly owned) handles technical management for part of the fleet.
- **HQ:** Glyfada, Greece (Marshall Islands incorporated)
- **Fiscal year end:** December 31

## 3. Revenue Model

[SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.B]: "As of the date of this report, all of our vessels are employed under long-term time charters which have a charter hire calculated at an index-linked rate based on the 5-routes T/C average of the BCI, while two of our vessels earn a daily hire of a fixed floor rate plus a profit-sharing scheme based on a significant premium over the daily BCI. All of our index-linked time charter agreements have the option to convert the floating rate into a fixed rate corresponding to the prevailing value of the respective Capesize FFAs." → Revenue is almost entirely index-linked to the Baltic Capesize Index (BCI), not fixed-rate or spot voyage — a deliberate strategic choice that ties SHIP's cash flow directly to Capesize freight-rate cyclicality.

[SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Risk Factor Summary]: "We are currently almost entirely dependent on index-linked charters, while in the past a part of our fleet was employed on a spot voyage basis. Any decrease in spot freight charter rates or indices in the future may adversely affect our earnings."

**TCE (time charter equivalent) reconciliation** [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 5]:

| | FY2023 | FY2024 | FY2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| TCE revenues ($000) | 104,185 | 161,584 | 149,995 |
| Operating days | 5,953 | 6,447 | 7,164 |
| **TCE rate ($/day)** | **17,501** | **25,063** | **20,937** |
| Fleet utilization | 99.1% | 98.9% | 96.3% |
| Daily vessel opex ($/day) | 6,879 | 6,976 | 7,127 |

TCE fell 16% YoY in FY2025 (from $25,063 to $20,937/day) even as operating days grew 11% (more fleet capacity), reflecting a softer Capesize rate environment — the classic dry bulk pattern where fleet growth and rate cyclicality move independently of each other.

## 4. Customer Base & Concentration

[SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.B]: "Customers individually accounting for more than 10% of our revenues" — anonymized as Customer A (32% FY2025 / 34% FY2024 / 28% FY2023), B (20%/22%/25%), C (17%/—/—), D (13%/12%/18%), E (—/—/12%). **Total top-customer concentration: 82% (FY2025) / 68% (FY2024) / 83% (FY2023)** — extreme concentration in the top 3-5 counterparties.

Named charterers appear at the individual-vessel level in the fleet employment table (not tied explicitly to the anonymized percentage table): Cargill, Glencore, NYK, Oldendorff, MOL, and Anglo American [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.B]. These are credible, investment-grade commodity trading houses and shipping majors — a genuine positive for counterparty credit quality, even though concentration itself is a real dependency risk.

## 6. Competitive Landscape

[SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.B]: "We compete for charters on the basis of price, vessel location, size, age and condition of the vessel, as well as on its reputation... We currently compete primarily with other owners of dry bulk vessels, many of which may have more resources than us and may operate vessels that are newer, and therefore more attractive to charterers than vessels we may operate. Ownership of dry bulk vessels is highly fragmented." → No named competitors anywhere in the filings, but a notable, explicit self-acknowledgment that rivals may operate **newer, more attractive tonnage** — directly relevant given SHIP's rising average fleet age (14.7 years).

[SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 3.D]: "Due to our lack of fleet diversification, adverse developments in the maritime dry bulk shipping industry would adversely affect our business, financial condition, and operating results." → Single-vessel-class concentration (Capesize/Newcastlemax only) is an explicit, company-acknowledged strategic risk.

## 7. Regulatory Environment

**Flag states:** Marshall Islands, Liberia, Portugal (per current fleet table).

**Emissions regulation:** [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.B]: EEXI (Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index) calculated for all vessels per MARPOL Annex VI; "EEXI, CII and rating values to the IMO DCS, became effective May 1, 2024." Nine of 20 vessels are scrubber-fitted [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.B — ESG Initiatives]; the remaining 12 comply via low-sulfur fuel. All newbuildings under construction are scrubber-fitted, indicating a deliberate shift toward full scrubber coverage.

**Sanctions:** [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 4.B]: "During the year ended December 31, 2025, none of our vessels called on ports located in countries subject at that time to comprehensive sanctions and embargoes imposed by the U.S... We believe that we are currently in compliance with all applicable sanctions and embargo laws and regulations."

**New/emerging risk — China port fees:** [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Risk Factor Summary]: "Recent actions by the U.S. and China imposing new port fees could have a material adverse effect on our operations and financial results." → Not present in the FY2023/FY2024 filings' risk summaries — a genuinely new development reflecting 2025-2026 US-China trade tension.

**PFIC risk:** [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 3.D]: flagged risk that Seanergy "may be classified as a passive foreign investment company (PFIC), which could result in adverse U.S. federal income tax consequences to U.S. holders" — a structural tax consideration specific to foreign shipping issuers.

## 8. Key Dependencies

1. **Index-linked (BCI) charter structure.** Nearly all revenue is tied to the Baltic Capesize Index — a deliberate strategy that removes negotiation risk on individual charters but leaves SHIP fully exposed to Capesize rate cyclicality, with only two vessels carrying a fixed-floor-plus-profit-share structure.

2. **Extreme customer concentration.** Top customers represent 68-83% of revenue across the three years reviewed — even with investment-grade counterparties (Cargill, Glencore, NYK, etc.), this is a structural dependency on a handful of commercial relationships.

3. **Related-party dependency on United Maritime Corporation.** [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 3.D]: "We depend on officers and directors who are associated with United Maritime Corporation... which may create conflicts of interest." Multiple 2025-2026 transactions (Dukeship bareboat-out, Squireship sale, a bridge loan) involve United, whose CEO is also Seanergy's CEO, Stamatios Tsantanis.

4. **Aging fleet relative to competitors.** Average fleet age of 14.7 years, rising each year in the period reviewed, against a competitive backdrop where the company itself acknowledges rivals "may operate vessels that are newer, and therefore more attractive to charterers."

5. **Third-party technical/crew management.** [SHIP_20F_FY2025.htm, Item 3.D]: "Our operational success partly depends upon V.Ships Greece's, Global Seaways', Navilands' and Fidelity's satisfactory performance" — a genuine operational dependency on outsourced managers for most of the fleet.

6. **Chinese leasing-company financing concentration.** A growing share of vessel financing runs through sale-and-leaseback arrangements with Chinese lessors (Huarong, BOCL), alongside traditional bank facilities (Alpha Bank, Piraeus Bank, Danish Ship Finance) — see [[Financial Deep Dive]] for detail.
